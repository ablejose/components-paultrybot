Part I

Foundations — the Why

Before a single bolt, we establish the ground truth of a poultry house: the biology, the chemistry, and the economics that make cleaning and watching non-negotiable. Everything the robot does later is an answer to a problem defined here.

Levels 1–10
LEVEL1

Part I · Foundations

The bird and the barn

Foundational why A modern broiler house is not a field — it is a tightly controlled box holding tens of thousands of fast-growing birds in a shared airspace. Understand that box and every later decision follows.

A broiler is a meat chicken bred to grow from a ~40 g chick to a ~2.5 kg bird in about six weeks. To make that economical, thousands are raised together on a littered floor inside a climate-controlled shed. The birds share the same air, the same water lines, the same floor, and the same droppings.

This shared environment is the single most important fact in the whole book. Because the birds cannot leave it and cannot clean it, the quality of that box is the quality of their life and the farm's profit. Two things silently degrade the box every hour: the birds foul the floor, and problems (a sickness, a leak, a dead bird) go unseen. The rest of Part I explains why each of those matters so much.

Builds on → nothing. This is the ground floor.
LEVEL2

Part I · Foundations

Why density creates fragility

Foundational why In a dense flock there is no such thing as a small problem. Anything bad multiplies through the whole population before a human would normally notice.

Put 20,000 birds in one airspace and every risk gains a multiplier. One sick bird breathes on hundreds. One leaking drinker wets a patch that gasses the whole end of the shed with ammonia. One dead bird becomes a disease seed the others peck at. Density turns a minor, local event into a flock-wide event with terrifying speed.

This is why the farm runs on prevention and early action, not repair. By the time damage is obvious, it is already large. The entire justification for constant cleaning and constant watching is this cascade — stop the small thing before density amplifies it.

LEVEL3

Part I · Foundations

Why litter exists

Foundational why The floor is covered in "litter" — a bed of dry material — on purpose. It is the farm's first and cheapest health technology.

Litter is a layer of absorbent bedding (wood shavings, chopped straw, rice hulls, or sawdust) spread across the floor. It does three jobs at once: it absorbs moisture from droppings and spilled water, it insulates the birds from the cold slab, and it gives them a natural surface to scratch and dust-bathe on (a welfare need).

But litter is a sponge, and a sponge only helps while it stays dry and loose. A fresh, friable bed protects the flock; a wet, compacted bed becomes the source of nearly every problem in the next four chapters. So litter is not just "the floor" — it is an active system that must be maintained. That maintenance is half of what the robot exists to do.

Builds on → Levels 1–2 (birds foul the shared floor).
LEVEL4

Part I · Foundations

Why moisture is the root enemy

Foundational why Almost every serious litter problem traces back to one variable: water. Control moisture and you pre-empt ammonia, caking, pathogens and footpad disease at once.

Healthy litter sits at roughly 25–35% moisture — damp enough to bind dust, dry enough to stay loose. Push it wetter (from droppings, humid air, or a leaking drinker) and three things happen: the bed compresses into hard "cake," it stops absorbing, and it becomes a chemical and biological reactor.

This is the pivot of the entire foundation. Moisture is upstream of ammonia (Level 5), of pathogens (Level 7), and of footpad burn (Level 6). A robot that keeps litter dry is not doing one job — it is switching off the source of several problems simultaneously. Remember this when we reach the scarifier in Part V: aeration is really moisture management.

Builds on → Level 3 (litter only works while dry).
LEVEL5

Part I · Foundations

Why ammonia forms

Foundational why Ammonia (NH₃) is the poultry house's signature poison, and it is a direct product of wet litter. Knowing the recipe tells you exactly how to prevent it.

Bird droppings are rich in nitrogen (uric acid and undigested protein). When litter is wet and warm, naturally present bacteria produce an enzyme called urease that breaks that nitrogen down — and one product is ammonia gas, which rises off the floor into the birds' breathing zone.

The recipe is the cure: NH₃ needs nitrogen + moisture + warmth + microbes. You cannot remove the droppings or the warmth, but you can remove the moisture. Dry the litter and the reaction slows dramatically. This is precisely why aeration (turning the bed so water evaporates) is the number-one cleaning function.

LEVEL6

Part I · Foundations

Why ammonia & dust harm birds

Foundational why Ammonia and airborne dust are not just unpleasant — they measurably cut growth, damage the birds, and cost money. This is what makes air quality a hard target.

Ammonia above ~20–25 ppm burns the eyes and scorches the lining of the respiratory tract, leaving birds open to infection, eating less, and growing slower — a direct hit to feed conversion ratio (FCR), the master efficiency number of the farm. Sit birds on wet, caustic litter and they also develop footpad dermatitis and hock burn: painful lesions that hurt welfare and downgrade the carcass at slaughter.

Dust adds a parallel injury. Fine particles of dried droppings, feather and feed carry pathogens deep into the lungs and irritate the airways of birds and workers. So "air quality" is really two linked enemies — gas and particulate — and both are worsened by the same wet, disturbed, poorly ventilated litter.

Builds on → Level 5 (ammonia) and Level 4 (moisture/dust).
LEVEL7

Part I · Foundations

Why pathogens thrive

Foundational why The same warm, moist, nutrient-rich litter that makes ammonia is a perfect incubator for disease — and disease in poultry runs in a self-feeding loop.

Bacteria (E. coli, Salmonella, Staphylococcus), coccidia parasites and viruses survive and multiply in damp litter and in the sticky "biofilm" that coats surfaces. Birds peck and scratch the floor constantly, so they ingest what is there, get infected, and shed even more pathogen back into the litter. Left alone, the pathogen load climbs with every cycle.

This loop is why disinfection exists and why houses get a deep clean-out between flocks: you must periodically break the cycle, or each new flock inherits a dirtier, more dangerous house than the last. It is also why avian-influenza precautions focus on manure and litter — the virus persists there for a long time.

LEVEL8

Part I · Foundations

Why dead birds are dangerous

Foundational why A dead bird is not a passive loss — it is an active hazard that must be removed fast. This single fact justifies a whole surveillance function later.

In any large flock some birds die daily; that is normal. The danger is leaving them. A carcass is a concentrated reservoir of whatever killed it, and it decays into the shared litter — feeding the disease loop from Level 7. Worse, live birds are naturally drawn to peck at a carcass, which spreads infection directly and can trigger cannibalistic pecking among the flock.

Mortality is also the farm's most important daily number. A sudden rise (industry practice flags anything over ~0.3% of the house in 24 hours) is the earliest hard signal of an outbreak. So dead-bird removal serves two masters: it stops a hazard, and it produces the data that everything else reacts to.

Builds on → Levels 2 & 7 (amplification + disease loop).
LEVEL9

Part I · Foundations

Why early detection wins

Foundational why Because problems amplify (Level 2), the value of information collapses with time. Catching a problem on day 1 instead of day 3 is the difference between an adjustment and a disaster.

Everything degrades on a curve: a disease front, an ammonia spike, a failing fan, a blocked water line. Detected early, each is a cheap correction. Detected late, each is a mass event. This is the economic engine behind surveillance — the earlier the signal, the smaller the loss.

The catch is that humans can only sample. A stockperson walks the house perhaps two or three times a day, taking snapshots. Between snapshots, the shed is unwatched. The gap between "how often problems start" and "how often we look" is exactly the opening a machine can close by watching continuously — the core case for the surveillance robot.

Builds on → Level 2 (amplification makes time expensive).
LEVEL10

Part I · Foundations

The two master jobs

Foundational why Everything in Levels 1–9 collapses into two continuous jobs. The whole robot is an attempt to do these two jobs better than a human can.

Reduce the foundation to its essence and only two duties remain, both feeding the one master goal — keep the pathogen load low and catch problems early:

1. Clean — control the environment: keep litter dry and aerated (Levels 4–5), keep the pathogen load down through disinfection (Level 7), and keep the air breathable (Level 6).
2. Watch — control the information: find dead and sick birds (Levels 8–9), and measure the air, litter and equipment continuously so problems surface on day one.

Hold onto this. Every component from Part IV onward exists to serve one of these two jobs. If a part cannot be traced back to "clean" or "watch," it does not belong on the robot.

Builds on → all of Part I. Sets up → Part II (how humans do these jobs today).
Part II

The Human Labor

Before automating a job you must understand it. Here we walk the two master jobs as they are actually done today — by hand — and, crucially, why each task is done the way it is. The limits we find at the end are the reason a robot is worth building.

Levels 11–16
LEVEL11

Part II · Human labor

The stockperson's day

The person who runs the birds — the stockperson — is the current implementation of "clean" and "watch." A typical day is a rhythm of walk-throughs: an early round to check overnight losses and set feed/water, mid-day and evening rounds to inspect birds and conditions, plus reactive tasks (fixing a leak, treating a sick pen) and constant record-keeping.

Why it's done this way The walk-through is the cheapest tool that combines all human senses at once — sight, sound, smell, touch — to judge a flock. A good stockperson can "read" a house in minutes. That richness is the bar the robot must eventually meet or exceed.

The chapters that follow break this day into its component tasks. For each we ask the same two questions the whole book turns on: what is done, and why.

Builds on → Level 10 (clean & watch). Sets up → Levels 12–15 (the tasks).
LEVEL12

Part II · Human labor

Manual litter management

What: Workers rake, hoe, or till the bedding to break up compacted "cake," especially wet patches around drinkers and feed lines. Between flocks, a de-caking machine or loader strips out the worst material.

Why it's done To keep litter dry and loose — which, from Levels 4–6, is what suppresses ammonia, footpad disease, dust and pathogens. Turning the bed exposes wet material to the air so moisture evaporates before it can gas off as NH₃.

Note what this task really is: it is moisture management dressed up as raking. The tool (a rake) is incidental; the goal is aeration and drying. When we build the scarifier in Level 29, we are automating this exact intent — not the rake, but the drying.

Builds on → Levels 4–5. Automated by → Level 29 (scarifier).
LEVEL13

Part II · Human labor

Manual cleaning & disinfection

What: Mostly during the empty "downtime" between flocks: remove all old litter and manure, dry-clean surfaces, wash with detergent and water, then spray, fog or fumigate disinfectant across every surface. Some farms also mist lightly while birds are present.

Why it's done To break the disease loop (Level 7). Removing organic matter first is essential because dirt shields microbes from disinfectant; the chemical step then kills the surviving pathogens, cutting the load the next flock inherits and reducing the need for antibiotics.

This is a hazardous, unpleasant job: workers are exposed to dust, ammonia and biocide in a confined space. That human cost is one more reason to hand it to a machine — the robot's nebuliser (Levels 31–32) automates the fogging step, and it can work the empty house overnight.

Builds on → Level 7. Automated by → Levels 31–32 (biocide system).
LEVEL14

Part II · Human labor

Manual health & mortality checks

What: On every round the stockperson removes dead birds, spots sick/lame/weak ones (isolating or humanely culling them), and reads the flock's body language — how birds are spread out, whether they are feeding, resting, huddling or panting.

Why it's done Dead birds are hazards and the key mortality signal (Level 8); early identification of the sick prevents an outbreak (Level 9). Bird distribution is a live thermometer: huddling means cold, edge-avoidance or panting means something is wrong.

This task is pure information-gathering, and it is exactly where continuous machine watching beats human sampling. A camera that never blinks can count mortality, map distribution, and flag a limping bird at 3 a.m. — the origin of the perception stack in Part VI.

Builds on → Levels 8–9. Automated by → Part VI (perception).
LEVEL15

Part II · Human labor

Manual environment & equipment checks

What: The worker reads thermometers and sensors, "feels" the air for heat and ammonia, checks that fans and inlets are working, and walks the lines looking for empty feeders and blocked or leaking nipple drinkers.

Why it's done Climate and air quality drive FCR and mortality (Level 6); a blocked feed line starves birds; and — closing a loop from Level 4 — a leaking drinker is the number-one cause of the wet litter that starts the whole ammonia/pathogen chain. Fixing the leak treats the cause, not just the symptom.

Fixed sensors help but only measure a few points. A human samples the rest by walking. A moving robot can map temperature, ammonia and moisture everywhere the birds live and spot the dripping nipple that a fixed sensor never sees (Part VII).

Builds on → Levels 4 & 6. Automated by → Part VII (environmental sensing).
LEVEL16

Part II · Human labor

Why manual labor can't scale

Foundational why The human method has four structural limits. Each one is a specification for the robot — a gap the machine is designed to fill.

1. It samples, it doesn't watch. Two or three rounds a day leave the house unobserved for hours, while problems start continuously (Level 9).
2. It's dangerous. Dust, ammonia and biocide make cleaning a genuine health hazard for workers (Level 13).
3. It doesn't measure precisely. "The air feels bad" is not a number; humans can't quantify ammonia or moisture across a whole house by hand.
4. It's labor-scarce and inconsistent. Skilled stockpeople are hard to find, and quality varies by person, fatigue and time of day.

Read those four limits as a design brief: build a machine that watches continuously, works where humans shouldn't, measures instead of guessing, and does it consistently. That brief is the rest of the book.

Part III · Labor → function

The translation principle

Foundational why Never automate a tool — automate the reason the tool exists. A robot that copies a rake is dumb; a robot that satisfies "keep litter dry" is smart.

The whole design method is one chain: for each manual task, name what is done, dig to why it is done, then design the function that satisfies that why — often by a completely different mechanism than the human uses.

LEVEL20

Part III · Labor → function

Why a ground robot (the Octopus model)

Foundational why The function sets decide the body. Because the cleaning functions must touch the litter, the machine must live on the floor — a ground robot, like the Octopus Biosafety design from France.

A ceiling-rail robot can watch beautifully, but it cannot aerate litter, cannot fog disinfectant into corners, and cannot reach a carcass — it has no hands on the floor. Since half our mandate is cleaning (Level 10), the platform must be a self-propelled ground vehicle that drives across the bed, carrying both the cleaning payload and the sensors.

That is the Octopus model we adopt: a low, all-terrain, self-recharging ground robot that scarifies litter and nebulises biocide while continuously mapping the barn — extended in this book with a full AI perception stack. Everything from Part IV on is the anatomy of that machine, built from the wheels up.

Part IV

Mechanics — the Body

Now we build the machine that carries everything. A body that survives a poultry house, crosses deep litter, and moves with precise, bird-safe control. Every choice here is dictated by the foundations: dust, moisture, ammonia and loose bedding.

Levels 21–28
LEVEL21

Part IV · Mechanics

The chassis: a frame for forces

Why it exists Everything else bolts to the chassis. It must hold the wheels, battery, sensors and a digging scarifier in fixed alignment while absorbing the forces of driving and tilling.

The chassis is the skeleton — typically a welded frame of aluminium (light, corrosion-resistant) or stainless steel (tougher, fully washable). Its job is rigidity: when the scarifier drags through packed litter, that reaction force must flow through a stiff frame, not twist the body or shake the cameras out of alignment.

It also sets the geometry that later chapters depend on — a low centre of gravity for stability (Level 25), and a profile low enough to pass under feed and drinker lines. Get the frame right and every subsystem has a stable home; get it wrong and no amount of clever software compensates.

Builds on → Level 20 (a ground robot needs a body).
LEVEL22

Part IV · Mechanics

Sealing, corrosion & washdown

Why it exists The house is full of the three things electronics hate most — dust, moisture and ammonia — and the robot must be pressure-washed between flocks for biosecurity. The body must keep all of that out.

Electronics live inside a sealed enclosure rated to an IP (Ingress Protection) standard — IP65 or better, meaning dust-tight and able to survive water jets. Gasketed panels and sealed cable glands form a continuous barrier. Ammonia is corrosive over time, so exposed metals are stainless or coated, and connectors are chosen for chemical resistance.

IP rating: two digits — the first is protection against solids (6 = dust-tight), the second against liquids (5 = water jets, 6 = powerful jets, 7 = immersion). "IP65" = fully dust-tight and jet-proof.

A small filtered fan can hold slight positive pressure inside the case so that any gap leaks air out rather than letting dust in — a simple trick that greatly extends electronics life.

Builds on → Levels 4–6 (dust, moisture, ammonia) & Level 7 (washdown/biosecurity).
LEVEL23

Part IV · Mechanics

Locomotion: wheels vs tracks

Why it matters The robot must cross deep, loose, uneven litter without bogging down or churning it into dust — the choice of what touches the ground decides whether it can move at all.

Wheels are simple, efficient and easy to seal — but small wheels sink into loose bedding. Tracks spread weight over a large area and climb obstacles well — but they trap litter, wear faster and cost more. For most broiler litter, large-diameter wheels with an aggressive tread are the pragmatic winner (the approach the Octopus-style robots use), reserving tracks for unusually deep or sloppy bedding.

The deciding factor is ground pressure — how hard the machine presses on the floor — which we quantify in the next level. Whatever the choice, low speed and gentle contact matter: the robot must move through the flock, not disturb it (Level 2's fragility).

Builds on → Level 3 (litter surface). Sets up → Level 24 (ground pressure).
LEVEL24

Part IV · Mechanics

Traction & ground pressure

Why it matters "Floating" on loose litter instead of digging in is a physics problem: spread the weight over enough contact area and the machine stays on top; concentrate it and it sinks.

Ground pressure = weight ÷ contact area. A heavy robot on small wheels has high pressure and ploughs a rut; the same robot on large wheels (or tracks) spreads its weight and rides the surface. Large-diameter wheels also roll over lumps more easily and give a bigger tread contact patch for traction — the grip that turns motor torque into forward motion instead of wheelspin.

Figure 24.1 — Same weight, different contact area. Bigger wheels = lower ground pressure = the robot floats instead of digging.

LEVEL25

Part IV · Mechanics

Suspension & stability

Why it exists Litter is lumpy. Without suspension, a wheel that lifts over a bump loses traction, and every jolt blurs the cameras the surveillance job depends on.

A simple passive suspension — springs or a rocker linkage at each wheel — lets wheels move independently so all of them stay loaded and gripping on uneven ground. It also isolates the sensor mast from shocks, keeping LiDAR and camera data clean (Part VI needs steady images to detect a limping bird).

Stability comes from geometry: a wide wheelbase and a low centre of gravity (heavy battery mounted low and central — see Level 46) keep the robot from tipping when it turns or crosses a ridge of cake. Slow speeds help too; there is never a reason for this machine to move fast.

Builds on → Levels 23–24. Serves → Part VI (steady sensing).
LEVEL26

Part IV · Mechanics

Drive motors: torque & gearing

Why it exists The robot needs force at very low speed — to push through litter and drag a scarifier — not top speed. That specific need dictates the motor and, above all, the gearbox.

A brushless DC (BLDC) motor spins efficiently and lasts long (no brushes to wear), but a fast-spinning motor has little turning force on its own. A gearbox fixes that: it trades speed for torque. Gear a motor down 30:1 and the output turns 30× slower but with roughly 30× the force — exactly the "slow and strong" profile a litter-crawler needs.

Figure 26.1 — Gear reduction: the small gear spins fast; the big gear turns slower but far stronger. Torque is what crosses litter.

Two independent geared motors (one per side) give differential drive — the steering method of Level 28.

Builds on → Level 24 (traction needs torque).
LEVEL27

Part IV · Mechanics

Motor drivers (ESCs)

Why it exists A microcontroller can only whisper tiny control signals; a motor needs to be fed big, precisely-timed gulps of battery current. The driver is the translator and muscle in between.

A motor driver (for BLDC motors, an ESC — electronic speed controller, e.g. an ODrive or VESC) takes a low-power command and switches high battery current to the motor coils using PWM (pulse-width modulation) — rapidly pulsing power on and off so the average sets the speed. Good drivers run a closed loop: they read the encoder (Level 28) and constantly correct to hold the exact commanded speed or torque, even as litter resistance changes.

Crucially, drivers also protect the system — limiting current so a stalled scarifier can't burn out a motor, and reporting faults up to the controller. They are where software intention becomes physical, controlled force.

Builds on → Level 26 (motors). Pairs with → Level 45 (the MCU).
LEVEL28

Part IV · Mechanics

Odometry: encoders & the IMU

Why it exists To act on a map (Part X) the robot must first know how far it has moved and which way it is facing. Odometry is that self-measurement — and it enables differential-drive steering.

An encoder on each drive motor counts shaft rotations: pulse rate gives speed, total count gives distance travelled by each wheel. Because the two wheels are driven independently (differential drive), the controller steers purely by speed difference — spin both wheels equally to go straight, drive one faster to curve, spin them opposite to turn in place (vital in a crowded house).

Wheels slip on loose litter, so encoders alone drift. An IMU (inertial measurement unit) — accelerometer + gyroscope + magnetometer — measures real motion and heading and is fused with the encoder data to correct that slip. Together they answer "where am I now?", the question SLAM (Level 48) turns into "where am I on the map?"

Builds on → Levels 26–27. Sets up → Level 48 (navigation).
Part V

The Cleaning Payload

Here the machine earns its keep. Two mechanisms — a litter scarifier and a biocide nebuliser — are the direct answers to the ammonia and pathogen problems from Part I. This is the heart of the Octopus model.

Levels 29–33
LEVEL29

Part V · Cleaning payload

The scarifier: mechanics of aeration

Why it exists This is the automation of Level 12 and the direct cure for Levels 4–5: a rotating tool that keeps litter dry and loose so ammonia never builds.

A scarifier is a powered drum or bar fitted with tines/rakes, slung under the rear of the robot and spun by its own geared motor. As the robot drives, the tines dig into the top layer of litter, lift it, and drop it — mixing air through the bed and breaking up compacted cake. Exposed to air, trapped moisture evaporates, and (from Level 5) the ammonia reaction slows right down.

Figure 29.1 — The scarifier turns caked, wet, ammonia-generating litter (left) into loose, aerated, drying litter (right).

Continuous light aeration on every patrol beats one hard manual pass a week: moisture never accumulates into cake in the first place, so ammonia stays low for the whole flock.

Builds on → Levels 5, 12. Needs → Level 30 (depth control).
LEVEL30

Part V · Cleaning payload

Depth control: the height actuator

Why it exists The scarifier must bite the litter at the right depth when working — but lift clear when turning, docking, or passing birds. Something has to raise and lower it on command.

A linear actuator — an electric motor turning a lead-screw that pushes a rod in and out — sets the drum's height. Extend it to press the tines a few centimetres into the bed; retract it to lift the tool for transit so it can't snag litter or startle birds. Depth can be tuned to litter condition: shallow on a good day, deeper into a stubborn cake.

This is also a safety and welfare feature: a spinning tool is only ever near the floor when it is meant to be working, and it lifts instantly if the robot needs to stop or reverse. Control of "when the tool touches" is as important as the tool itself.

Builds on → Level 29. Commanded by → Level 45 (MCU) & Level 33 (timing).
LEVEL31

Part V · Cleaning payload

The biocide system: tank & pump

Why it exists This is the automation of Level 13 and the tool against Level 7's disease loop: the robot must carry and deliver disinfectant on its own.

Onboard sits a food-grade tank (5–10 L) of disinfectant with a level sensor so the robot knows when to alert for a refill. A pump — a diaphragm or peristaltic type, chosen because they handle chemicals well and self-prime — draws liquid from the tank and pressurises it toward the spray nozzles. The pump rate sets how much biocide is laid down per metre travelled.

Two modes matter: a light treatment while birds are present (bird-safe products, gentle dosing), and a heavy empty-house decontamination during downtime, when the robot can fog the whole shed unattended overnight — doing the most hazardous human job (Level 13) with no human in the room.

Builds on → Levels 7, 13. Delivers via → Level 32 (nozzles).
LEVEL32

Part V · Cleaning payload

Nebulisation: droplet physics

Why it matters How the disinfectant is broken up decides whether it works. Too coarse and it soaks the litter (adding the moisture we fight in Level 4); too fine and it drifts away. The sweet spot is a fog.

Nebulisation (fogging) forces the liquid through fine nozzles that atomise it into very small droplets — a mist that floats briefly and settles evenly over surfaces and litter without wetting them through. Even coverage means every surface gets disinfectant contact; low added moisture means you don't undo the drying work of the scarifier.

Figure 32.1 — Droplet size is everything: a fine fog coats surfaces uniformly without re-wetting the litter you just dried.

Bonus: a fine mist also knocks airborne dust out of the air (Level 6), so one mechanism serves disinfection and dust control.

Builds on → Levels 4, 6, 31.
LEVEL33

Part V · Cleaning payload

Payload integration & timing

Why it matters Two cleaning tools plus a moving vehicle only help if they act at the right place and time. Integration is what turns parts into a function.

The payload must be choreographed with driving. The robot lowers and runs the scarifier only while patrolling litter, lifts it to turn or dock, and doses biocide at a rate matched to its ground speed so coverage stays even. When perception (Part VI) flags a wet spot, the robot slows and works that patch harder — targeted aeration exactly where Level 4's problem is worst.

This coordination is why the robot needs a real-time controller (Level 45) beneath its AI brain (Level 44): tool timing, speed and safety must be deterministic. The payload is not a bolt-on; it is woven into the motion-control loop.

Part VI

Perception — the Senses

To clean intelligently and to watch the flock, the robot needs senses. This part builds its eyes — for driving safely and for the AI surveillance that replaces the human's daily inspection.

Levels 34–39
LEVEL34

Part VI · Perception

Sensing = two jobs at once

Foundational why Every sensor on this robot pulls double duty: it keeps the machine from hurting birds or itself, and it feeds the surveillance mission. Understanding this dual role explains the whole sensor suite.

Job one is navigation & safety: don't hit a wall, a feed line, a person, or a bird. Job two is surveillance: find dead and sick birds, read distribution, spot faults — the automation of Levels 14–15. The clever part of the design is that the same cameras and rangefinders serve both.

No single sensor is enough, so we use several that fail in different conditions and fuse them — LiDAR for geometry in the dark and dust, cameras for rich detail, thermal for heat and night, proximity as a last-resort backstop. The next chapters take them one at a time.

Builds on → Levels 14–15. Feeds → Part VIII (the brain).
LEVEL35

Part VI · Perception

LiDAR: seeing with light

Why it exists The robot needs reliable distance measurement that works in darkness and dust, where cameras struggle. LiDAR provides the geometric backbone for navigation.

LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) fires rapid laser pulses and times how long each reflection takes to return. Because light travels at a known speed, time-of-flight converts directly to distance. Sweeping the beam around builds a precise 2-D or 3-D map of walls, equipment and obstacles — dozens of times a second, in the dark, through airborne dust.

Figure 35.1 — Time-of-flight ranging: measure how long light takes to bounce back, and you know the distance.
Builds on → Level 34. Feeds → Level 48 (SLAM).
LEVEL36

Part VI · Perception

Depth & stereo cameras

Why it exists LiDAR gives sparse geometry; the robot also needs dense, per-pixel distance to judge the exact shape and position of birds, wet spots and small obstacles right in front of it.

A stereo (depth) camera uses two lenses a known distance apart, exactly like human eyes. Software compares the two images and measures the shift (disparity) of each feature between them — near objects shift a lot, far objects a little — and converts that into a distance for every pixel. The result is a 3-D "point cloud" of the scene ahead.

That dense depth is what lets the robot avoid stepping on a bird, gauge the height of a cake ridge for the scarifier, and give the AI models a 3-D sense of where a detected carcass actually lies on the floor. It complements LiDAR: richer and closer-range, but more affected by darkness and dust — which is why we keep both.

Builds on → Levels 34–35. Feeds → Levels 44, 48.
LEVEL37

Part VI · Perception

RGB vision & the AI eye

Why it exists This is the sensor that replaces the stockperson's eyes (Level 14). Colour images are what the AI models read to find dead birds, judge distribution, and spot equipment faults.

A high-resolution RGB camera captures ordinary colour frames. A global shutter (capturing the whole frame at one instant) is preferred so images don't smear while the robot moves. On its own the camera just sees pixels — the intelligence comes in Part VIII, where neural networks turn those pixels into "that's a dead bird," "birds are avoiding this corner," or "this nipple drinker is leaking."

Because it is mobile and tireless, this eye does what a human round cannot: it inspects the whole house continuously, quantifies what it sees, and logs it — turning subjective "the flock looks off" into measured, time-stamped data (Level 9's early-detection payoff).

Builds on → Levels 14, 34. Interpreted by → Level 44.
LEVEL38

Part VI · Perception

Thermal imaging

Why it exists Houses are often dark, and heat carries health information a colour camera can't see. Thermal vision lets the robot work and watch around the clock.

A thermal camera uses a microbolometer — a grid of tiny elements whose electrical resistance changes with the infrared (heat) radiation falling on them — to build a temperature image. It needs no light at all, so night patrols are as effective as day. Heat patterns also add clues: a cold spot where birds avoid a draught, an abnormally cold carcass, or hot zones with poor airflow.

Fused with the RGB camera, thermal makes detections more robust: a shape that is bird-sized and the wrong temperature is a confident dead-bird call. It is a second, independent way of seeing — valuable precisely because it fails differently from ordinary cameras.

Builds on → Levels 34, 37. Feeds → Level 44.
LEVEL39

Part VI · Perception

Proximity, bumpers & the safety net

Why it exists Smart sensors can be fooled by dust, glare or an unexpected bird. A simple, independent safety layer must catch what the clever sensors miss — because a mistake here hurts a living animal.

A ring of short-range ultrasonic or time-of-flight sensors gives a last-metre distance check all around the robot, and physical bumper switches cut drive power the instant anything is actually touched. These are deliberately dumb and reliable — they don't reason, they just stop the machine.

This defence-in-depth is a core safety principle: never trust one system with a life. The AI plans, LiDAR and cameras guide, but the bumper is the reflex that always works. It is the physical embodiment of the bird-safety promise the whole design makes (expanded in Level 49).

Builds on → Level 34. Expanded in → Level 49 (safety).
Part VII

Environmental Sensing

The robot's second surveillance gift: because it lives on the floor, it measures the air, litter and sound exactly where the birds do. These are the numbers a human can only guess at.

Levels 40–42
LEVEL40

Part VII · Environment

Gas sensing: ammonia & CO₂

Why it exists Ammonia (Levels 5–6) is the invisible enemy and CO₂ reveals ventilation health. A floor robot measures both at bird height, where fixed ceiling sensors are blind.

An electrochemical NH₃ cell senses ammonia: the gas reacts at an internal electrode and produces a small electric current proportional to concentration — a direct ppm reading. A CO₂ sensor uses NDIR (non-dispersive infrared): CO₂ absorbs a specific infrared wavelength, so measuring how much of that light is absorbed gives the concentration.

The power is in mapping. As the robot roams, it builds a heat-map of ammonia and CO₂ across the whole house, exposing the low-lying ammonia pocket by a leaky drinker or the stagnant corner a fixed sensor never reaches. This is Level 15's manual "feel the air," turned into precise, located numbers.

Builds on → Levels 5–6, 15.
LEVEL41

Part VII · Environment

Climate & dust: temp, humidity, PM

Why it exists Temperature drives FCR and comfort; humidity predicts litter caking; dust is a respiratory hazard. All three shape bird health from Part I and need continuous, mapped measurement.

A combined temperature/humidity sensor (e.g. Sensirion SHT-series) uses tiny elements whose electrical properties change with heat and moisture, digitised to °C and %RH. A PM (particulate) sensor shines a laser across an air sample and counts the light scattered by passing dust particles to estimate how much is in the air.

Humidity is the early-warning cousin of litter moisture (Level 4): rising %RH foretells caking before it happens, letting the robot pre-emptively aerate. Dust maps tell the farmer when to adjust ventilation and confirm whether the fogging (Level 32) is knocking particulate down.

Builds on → Levels 4, 6. Acts through → Levels 29, 32.
LEVEL42

Part VII · Environment

Litter moisture, light & sound

Why it exists Three more senses close the loop: measure moisture at its source, verify the lighting program, and hear disease before it's visible.

A rear-mounted capacitive moisture probe reads litter wetness directly — water changes the dielectric between its prongs, and capacitance maps to % moisture. This is the most upstream signal in the whole system (Level 4), letting the robot target aeration exactly where it's needed. A light sensor checks the lighting program that governs bird behaviour and growth.

Most powerfully, a microphone array plus audio AI lets the robot listen: flocks with respiratory disease develop characteristic rales and sneezes, and stress changes the soundscape — often before anything is visible. Multiple mics also give direction, pointing to where in the house the sound comes from.

Builds on → Levels 4, 14. Interpreted by → Level 44.
Part VIII

The Brain

Senses are useless without something to interpret and act. Here we give the robot two minds — one clever, one dependable — and the intelligence that turns pixels and sound into decisions.

Levels 43–45
LEVEL43

Part VIII · The brain

Two brains: smart vs safe

Foundational why Heavy AI is powerful but unpredictable in timing; safety needs guaranteed, instant response. You cannot get both from one processor, so the robot uses two — each doing what it's best at.

A high-power AI computer handles perception, mapping and planning — big, occasionally slow, "thinking" work. A separate real-time microcontroller (MCU) owns motors, the scarifier, the pump and the emergency stop, running a fixed control loop that always responds within milliseconds. The AI proposes; the MCU disposes — and the MCU can stop the machine even if the AI hangs.

LEVEL44

Part VIII · The brain

Edge AI: how machines recognise

Why it exists This is the intelligence that replaces the stockperson's trained judgement (Level 14) — turning camera and microphone data into "dead bird here," "lame bird there," "this drinker leaks."

A neural network is a model trained on thousands of labelled examples until it learns the visual and audio patterns of each target. Shown a new frame, it outputs detections with confidence scores. Running these models on the robot ("edge AI") rather than in the cloud means decisions don't depend on the network and happen in real time — essential in a metal shed with patchy connectivity.

Why a GPU: neural networks are millions of small multiplications done at once. A computer like the NVIDIA Jetson Orin has a GPU built to run those in parallel, so it can analyse live video on battery power.

The model suite mirrors the surveillance function set (Level 19): dead-bird, gait/lameness, distribution heat-map, drinker/feeder fault, wet-litter, plus an audio model for respiratory/stress sounds. Their outputs become alerts and records (Level 48).

Builds on → Levels 19, 37–38, 42.
LEVEL45

Part VIII · The brain

The real-time MCU & buses

Why it exists The safety-critical, precisely-timed jobs — motor control, tool timing, reading the E-stop — must never wait for the AI. A dedicated microcontroller guarantees that.

The MCU (e.g. an STM32 or Teensy) runs a tight, deterministic loop: read encoders, IMU and gas sensors; command the motor drivers, scarifier and pump; watch the bumpers and E-stop. Because it does only this, its response time is guaranteed — the foundation of collaborative safety (Level 49).

Everything talks over robust internal buses: CAN bus carries short, noise-resistant control messages between the MCU, drivers and sensors (it was built for exactly this in vehicles), while USB3 handles the heavy camera and LiDAR data into the AI computer. Choosing buses that survive electrical noise matters in a shed full of motors and fans.

Part IX

Power

None of the above runs without energy — safely stored, cleanly distributed, and replenished without a human. Power is the quiet system that decides whether the robot can truly work 24/7.

Levels 46–47
LEVEL46

Part IX · Power

Energy: battery chemistry & the BMS

Why it exists The robot needs lots of energy for motors and a digging scarifier, in a hot, dusty, enclosed building full of live animals — so the battery must be high-current and exceptionally safe.

The chosen chemistry is LiFePO₄ (lithium iron phosphate): it delivers high current, lasts many hundreds of charge cycles, and — unlike ordinary lithium-ion — is highly resistant to thermal runaway (fire). In a barn you cannot afford a battery that might ignite, so this trade of a little energy density for a lot of safety is the right one.

Every pack is guarded by a BMS (battery management system): it monitors each cell's voltage and temperature, balances them, and cuts the circuit if anything exceeds a safe limit (over-charge, over-current, over-heat). The BMS is why the pack lasts and why it's safe. Mounted low and central, this heavy pack also gives the robot its stability (Level 25).

Builds on → Levels 25–26 (torque draws current). Distributed by → Level 47.
LEVEL47

Part IX · Power

Distribution & self-docking

Why it exists Different subsystems need different voltages, the machine must be killable instantly, and — to be truly autonomous — it must recharge itself without anyone lifting a finger.

The pack feeds a 24 V main bus. Motors take that directly; DC-DC converters step it down to clean 12 V (LiDAR, pump, actuator) and 5 V (AI computer, MCU, sensors) rails. Between the pack and the motor bus sits the E-stop / contactor — a hardware switch that physically cuts motor power regardless of software.

Part X · Autonomy

Navigation: SLAM & path planning

Why it exists To clean and watch the whole house on its own, the robot must know where it is, remember where it has been, and decide where to go next — with no human driving.

SLAM (Simultaneous Localisation and Mapping) fuses LiDAR (Level 35), depth (Level 36) and odometry (Level 28) to do two things at once: build a map of the house and track its own position on that map in real time. With a map, a path planner can lay out efficient coverage patrols (so every square metre gets scarified and scanned), route around obstacles, and find its way home to the dock.

LEVEL49

Part X · Autonomy

Safety, biosecurity & working with life

Foundational why This machine works among living animals and people, and its whole purpose is to reduce disease. If it stresses birds, injures someone, or spreads pathogens, it fails — no matter how clever it is.

Bird & human safety: the robot moves slowly and predictably, and (via person/animal detection, Level 39, plus the MCU's guaranteed reflexes, Level 45) slows or stops near people and dense birds. It should carry collaborative-safety certification — the same standard as robots designed to share space with humans. The E-stop and bumpers are the hardware backstop that always works.

Biosecurity: the robot must never become a disease vector. One robot stays in one house (moving between houses would carry pathogens, undoing Level 7's whole point), and its wheels/tools are cleaned at the dock (a disinfectant tray or UV step). A status beacon and buzzer tell staff what it's doing. Safety here isn't a feature bolted on — it's a precondition of the mission.

Build it in this order

  1. Rolling chassis (IV) Frame, wheels, motors, drivers, battery, BMS, E-stop — get safe manual driving over litter first.
  2. Motion control (IV) Add MCU, encoders, IMU; tune closed-loop crawl speed and turn-in-place.
  3. Perception (VI) Mount LiDAR + cameras on the AI computer; bring up SLAM and obstacle avoidance.
  4. Environment sensing (VII) Add the gas/climate/moisture/mic pod; start logging bird-level maps.
  5. Cleaning payload (V) Fit the scarifier + actuator, then the tank, pump and fog nozzles.
  6. Autonomy & docking (IX–X) Add the dock, homing, safe-speed, and dock-and-go patrols.
  7. AI surveillance (VIII) Deploy the detection models + audio health; wire up alerts and records.
Priority: lead with surveillance + litter aeration — highest value, lowest risk. Add biocide fogging and any bird/egg pickup last: they are the hardest and most safety-sensitive. That is how the foundations (Part I) tell you to sequence the build.
